- **`Keys.hmac` overwrites its copies of the key** (#2120, from the review of #1481): the HMAC routine of both engines, `una_interp::hmac_sha256`, copied the key to a heap vector and made two more, the key under each pad, and freed the three without a wipe, with the states of its hashes. It now holds the padded key, its two pads (one buffer, changed in place), the hash state and the inner digest in fixed arrays on its stack, with no allocation but the result, and overwrites all of them with zeros before it returns; for a key longer than the block of the hash, the key's digest and the last bytes of the key that the padding copies are in the same arrays. The computation and the wipe are one function of a private module, so no code of the crate can compute the MAC without the wipe. It is not a volatile write: the zeros are written with `fill(0)` and kept by `std::hint::black_box`, as in `una_project::registry::keyfile::wipe`; `una-interp` forbids `unsafe`, which a volatile write needs. ADR 0133 rule 7 calls such a wipe removable by an optimizer. The zeros were measured to be written on aarch64-apple-darwin with the pinned toolchain 1.98.1, in the release and dev profiles; this was not measured on Linux or x86-64, and no test in the tree can observe it. A dependency feature and own hash code: `una-interp` and `una-rt` gain no dependency (`Cargo.lock` is unchanged, and neither depends on `una-crypto` or `zeroize`), but `una-interp` now enables the feature `compress` of `sha2`, which makes the SHA-256 compression function a public name for the whole build, and the padding and length encoding of SHA-256 are now the project's own code in both engines, where they were `sha2`'s hasher before (`docs/security/tcb.md` §3.4). Not overwritten, as before: what the compression function of `sha2` keeps in its own frame during a call, which in its software path (every aarch64 target, and x86-64 without the SHA extensions) is a copy of the chaining value, key-equivalent after the block of a pad, and a message schedule that holds the key under the pad; copies a compiler makes in registers; the keys in the run's table for the whole run; the key file's text and its decoding at start-up; and the bearer key that the operations of `Http` copy out of the table into the request they send. Results are bit-identical: the seven test cases of RFC 4231 and literal known answers for the empty key, the empty message and a key of one block pass on the routine, the cases whose data is a text pass through `Keys.hmac` of the native runtime, and the routine equals the former one over keys and data of every length around the block. No behavior of a program changes.
